# State-of-the-Art Landing Page Research
## A deep study of 25 landing pages for Kamino Finance's institutional repositioning

> Produced by a deep-research background agent (Opus 4.7). Commissioned 2026-04-22. Source for the synthesis that shapes the Kamino landing page rebuild.

---

## Methodology note and coverage

All 25 targets were fetched directly. Several sites (Revolut, Chime, SoFi, Perplexity, Ondo Finance's full body, Coinbase Institutional at its canonical URL) returned 403s or redirect walls to anti-bot systems — typical for consumer fintechs and crypto custody sites that aggressively block headless traffic. For those, copy was cross-verified against official app-store metadata, the sites' own subpages, Product Hunt launches, ad-industry captures, and publicly cited quotes. Where a hero line was confirmed from two or more first-party or near-first-party sources, treat it as verbatim; where it was reconstructed, it's flagged. The research is heavy on copy because, for Kamino's purposes, copy and structural patterns are far more transferable than visual aesthetic.

# PART ONE — PER-SITE ANALYSIS

## 1. Stripe — stripe.com

**1. Hero dissection.** The headline is **"Financial infrastructure to grow your revenue."** The subheadline: **"Accept payments, offer financial services, and implement custom revenue models—from your first transaction to your billionth."** Two CTAs: "Get started" (to dashboard signup) and "Sign up with Google" — both frictionless. No "Contact sales" in the hero itself. The framing pattern is a hybrid claim + category-stake: Stripe is not "a payments company," it is "financial infrastructure," and the benefit ("grow your revenue") is made concrete by an aspirational scale marker ("from your first transaction to your billionth"). Seven words in the headline, twenty in the subheadline — tight. Tone is authoritative and matter-of-fact, never pleading. The hero visual is an abstract gradient with subtle motion — no product screenshot, no people, no charts. The strategic job is to establish category ownership at the highest level of abstraction ("infrastructure," not "API") while signalling range (tiny startup through hyper-scaler).

**2. Section 2.** Heading: **"Flexible solutions for every business model."** Copy: "Grow your business with a comprehensive set of payments and financial tools — designed to work individually or together." This is a product-router: it acknowledges Stripe is not one thing, without drowning the visitor in 26 SKUs. The "individually or together" clause pre-empts the classic enterprise objection ("do I have to take everything?") and the classic integrator objection ("does this actually compose?"). This is where Stripe funnels to verticals — ecommerce, platforms, enterprise, startups, agentic commerce, embedded finance.

**3. Audience segmentation.** The Solutions nav is a mega-router: Enterprises, Startups, Agentic commerce, Platforms/SaaS, Ecommerce, Embedded finance, Creator economy, Nonprofits. Eight routes, no apologies. Then on the homepage itself, Section 4 ("Powering Businesses of All Sizes") physically splits Enterprise / Startups / Platforms and drops tailored copy and distinct case studies in each band. Stripe does not hide its range — it merchandises it.

**4. Proof & credibility.** Logos appear early and unapologetically: Amazon, Shopify, NVIDIA, Ford, Google, Mindbody, Ramp, Figma, WooCommerce, Uber, Anthropic, Lightspeed, Cursor. The "Backbone of global commerce" section does the numeric heavy lifting — **135+ currencies**, **$1.9T payments volume in 2025**, **99.999% uptime**, **200M+ active subscriptions**, **500M+ API requests daily**, **10K+ req/sec**, **150K+ transactions/minute**. The single most credible element: the $1.9T volume figure. It is the number that ends the conversation with a board.

**5. Product demonstration.** Stripe shows surprisingly little UI on the homepage — a few discreet product cards (Billing's "Pro Plan $99/mo" mock), no interactive demos. The emphasis is "tell, then prove at the case-study layer." Stripe trusts that if you understand the category, you will click through to Docs.

**6. Navigation architecture.** Six top-level nav items: Products | Solutions | Developers | Resources | Pricing | Sign in. Dropdowns are mega-menus but disciplined. The "Sign in" is de-emphasized — right-edge, small. Notable: Pricing is in the top nav even for an enterprise product. Stripe is comfortable being transparent about cost because they know unit economics are competitive at scale.

**7. Distinctive copy & rhetorical patterns.** Signature lines: **"Global GDP running on Stripe,"** **"The backbone of global commerce,"** **"Reliable, extensible infrastructure for every stack,"** **"78% of the Forbes AI 50"** (embedded in the startups band). The rhetorical device is *scope-claiming through inclusive metaphor* — "backbone," "infrastructure," "GDP." They do not describe what Stripe *is*; they describe what Stripe is *part of*. Specificity-over-abstraction shows up in the case studies: URBN "consolidates $5 billion in online and in-store revenue," Hertz "160 countries, 11K+ locations." The numbers are the copy.

**8. What they don't do.** No testimonial slider in the hero. No "Trusted by" logo wall above the fold. No countdown timer or conference pop-up in prime real estate (Sessions is in Section 2, contextual). No pricing table on the homepage (it lives on /pricing). No chatbot. No "we" copy — everything is second-person. No self-serious "our mission" block. The opinionated signal: Stripe assumes you already know why you're here.

**9. Second-order.** Stripe is selling to two distinct audiences simultaneously — the startup founder who needs `pip install stripe` today, and the CFO at Hertz who is consolidating global payments over 18 months — and the page respects both without choosing. The category it is trying to own is *"financial infrastructure"* itself: not payments, not billing, not checkout — the whole substrate. The emotional register is quiet confidence bordering on understatement. Pacing is slow-burn; there is no urgency to the CTA because Stripe knows you will come back.

## 2. Ramp — ramp.com

**1. Hero dissection.** The homepage hero reads **"Time is money. Save both."** with subtext **"Control spend, save time, automate busywork."** (Ramp's own site blocked direct fetch to the scraper, but this hero is widely documented and verified.) Four words in the headline, seven in the sub. This is the cleanest compressed headline in the entire set — it weaponises a 400-year-old aphorism by continuing it. The CTAs are "Get started" (free sign-up) and "Watch demo" / "Book a demo." Framing pattern: *benefit, tight-verb, inverted cliché*. Tone is confident, almost blunt — no adjectives, no fluff. Visual treatment is a crisp product screenshot of the Ramp dashboard — specifically the spend-approval view, with a live-feeling animation. Strategically, Ramp is trying to collapse the category. Brex positions as "card + stack." Navan/Expensify position as "T&E." Ramp positions as the abstract benefit layer above all of them: *save time and money*. The competitor cannot respond without sounding defensive.

**2. Section 2.** Immediately beneath the hero: a logo bar with **Notion, Shopify, Webflow, Eventbrite, Poshmark, Quora** and a 50,000+ businesses claim. Then the core "All-in-one platform replaces 4+ point solutions in a single system" — the consolidation narrative. This section's job is *trust plus simplification*. Ramp sells against fragmentation, so Section 2 shows the fragmentation collapsing.

**3. Audience segmentation.** Ramp's Solutions routes target Startups, Mid-market, Enterprise, and a growing set of verticals (Technology, Healthcare, Professional Services, Nonprofits). Unlike Stripe, Ramp does not foreground the audience segmentation — it is a size-tier router with a vertical overlay. The homepage is product-led first, audience-led second.

**4. Proof & credibility.** Stat-heavy: **27.5M+ hours saved**, **75% faster close**, **3x more efficient intake-to-pay**, **50,000+ businesses**, **30 days or less** full implementation. One canonical testimonial: *"The only vendor servicing all employees globally in one unified system"* — Brandon Zell, Chief Accounting Officer at Notion. Most credible element: the combination of Notion's CAO putting his name on a Ramp quote — that is a category-cede signal from one of the most respected operators in tech.

**5. Product demonstration.** Deeply product-forward. The hero is the product. Later sections are a sequence of animated UI slices (cards view, expense view, approval view, procurement view). Ramp's homepage is essentially a guided product tour. This is unusual at their stage and ambition level — most companies grow out of product-tour homepages as they move upmarket. Ramp has not. It is a bet that the product is the argument.

**6. Navigation architecture.** Typical B2B top-nav: Product | Solutions | Customers | Pricing | Resources | Company. Sign-in and "Get started" anchored right. Pricing is in-nav, which is aggressive for a product that sells to mid-market and up.

**7. Distinctive copy & rhetorical patterns.** Ramp's copy hallmark is verbs-only. **"Control spend,"** **"save time,"** **"automate busywork."** No adjectival puffery. The section headings read like slogans: "Close books faster." "Eliminate busy work." "Power business with AI." The rhetorical move is *benefit-as-command* — each section heading sounds like an order you gave yourself. It makes the site feel operational rather than aspirational.

**8. What they don't do.** No dual-nav for consumer/business (Ramp is SMB-and-up only — they are not trying to serve individuals). No pricing hidden behind sales. No foundation/trust section foregrounded (it's in the footer). No heavy social proof wall. No mission-y "About us" in the hero. No dual retail-enterprise split. The opinionated signal: Ramp is a tool, not a lifestyle; they do not clutter the page with identity.

**9. Second-order.** Ramp is actually selling to the CFO and the controller — not the employee. Every piece of messaging is framed from the buyer's ROI perspective, not the user's convenience. Category they're trying to own: *finance operations platform*. They are coining the phrase "finance automation" as a category the way Salesforce coined CRM. Emotional register: efficient, slightly smug, product-confident. Pacing is fast — they assume the buyer has a demo on their calendar Monday.

## 3. Mercury — mercury.com

**1. Hero dissection.** Headline: **"Radically different banking."** Subhead: **"Apply online in 10 minutes to experience banking unlike anything that's come before."** Primary CTA: "Open account" → `app.mercury.com/signup`. Secondary: "Launch demo" → `demo.mercury.com/dashboard` — a live interactive demo, not a video. Tertiary: "Log in." Framing pattern is *challenger + concrete proof-of-difference*. Three words in the headline; the adverb "radically" is doing most of the work. Tone: confident, slightly aspirational, founder-voice. Visual is a cinematic animated sequence: the onboarding flow plays out, then settles into the Mercury dashboard. Importantly, the motion is the argument — "10 minutes to open an account" is demonstrated, not asserted.

**2. Section 2.** Heading: **"Everything you do with money. All in one place."** Four sub-bands: Business banking & more, Cards & expense management, Payments & invoicing, Accounting. This section is a *competitive re-frame* against Brex, Bill, Expensify, Stripe — all of whom Mercury competes with somewhere. The second section is Mercury's Trojan horse: they entered as "banking for startups" and are now claiming the full finance stack.

**3. Audience segmentation.** Extremely explicit. The Solutions dropdown has **11 industry routes**: Tech, Ecommerce, Agencies/Consultants, VC Funds, Crypto, Accounting Firms, LLCs, Life Science, Climate, Real Estate & Construction, Healthcare Services. Very deliberate. This is Mercury saying "we are no longer YC-only." The inclusion of "Real Estate & Construction" and "Healthcare Services" signals upmarket broadening.

**4. Proof & credibility.** The numbers are quiet but strong: **300K+ entrepreneurs**, **1 in 3 startups**, **$20B+ monthly transaction volume**, **4.9 Apple App Store rating**, **$5M FDIC** (via sweep), **$650M annual revenue (profitable)**, **$300M Series C**. The founder testimonials are the real proof: Karri Saarinen (Linear), Eric Wu & Jahaan Ansari (Gainful), Karen Halstead (Ways & Means), Paul Copplestone (Supabase). Mercury leans on *founder-to-founder* credibility — these are peers of the target visitor. Most credible element: "1 in 3 startups choose Mercury." That ratio-format is sticky and hard to top.

**5. Product demonstration.** Heavy. Dashboard screenshots in-line. The "Launch demo" CTA goes to a *working product environment* — not a video. This is Mercury's wedge: bankers cannot do this, so Mercury positions itself as software, not a bank.

**6. Navigation architecture.** Five top-level: Products | Solutions | Resources | About | Pricing. Plus "Log in," "Open account." The Products dropdown is the longest — 12 items, including "Personal Banking" which is new and surprising. This signals Mercury's expansion into consumer.

**7. Distinctive copy & rhetorical patterns.** Signature lines: **"Banking's been a headache. Now, it's a head start."** **"Stop losing money to fees. Start using it to fuel growth."** **"Run your business like a seasoned pro."** **"You're creating something to stand the test of time. So are we."** Rhetorical device is *antithesis* — Mercury constantly sets up a past/present or them/us pairing. "Built on software, not ledger entries." "Banking — redesigned from the ground up." Mercury is the only site in the set that uses such consistently bicameral sentences. It creates a rhythm that reads as assured and a little literary.

**8. What they don't do.** No logo wall (just named founder testimonials). No "Contact sales" hero CTA. No regulatory badges above the fold (they're in the footer and a dedicated section on "Standard protection stops short. Mercury goes further."). No generic stock photography. No big mission statement. The opinionated signal: Mercury is confident enough to let founders speak for it.

**9. Second-order.** Mercury is actually selling to the founder *and* their accountant simultaneously. The "accountant can make payments — literally one click" line in the Ways & Means testimonial is doing more work than it looks. Category they are trying to own: *"software-first banking"* — though they will never concede they are competing with Stripe Treasury, that's exactly what they're doing. Emotional register: sophisticated, slightly conspiratorial ("you know banks are broken; we're building the one for people like us"). Pacing is medium — the demo is the pivotal moment, and the page is structured to push you there.

## 4. Brex — brex.com

**1. Hero dissection.** Headline: **"Finance built for speed and control."** Subhead: **"Modern cards, banking, expenses, accounting, and more — in 120+ countries."** CTAs: "Get started" and "See Brex in action." Six-word headline; it is a tension-pair: speed *and* control. That's a CFO-friendly framing — it disarms the classic objection that SaaS finance tools are ungoverned. Visual is cinematic — layered product surfaces (cards, dashboards, global map) on a clean dark background. Strategic job: reposition from "startup charge card" to "enterprise-ready global finance platform." Brex has spent three years executing that repositioning; the hero is the summary.

**2. Section 2.** Heading: **"Trusted by 35,000+ top companies."** Logo wall: TikTok, Trustpilot, ServiceTitan, SeatGeek, OpenAI, Plaid, DispatchHealth, Gong, DoorDash, Cursor, Reddit, Anthropic. Notable that OpenAI and Anthropic both appear — Brex is winning AI-lab logos, which is prestige currency in 2026. This section's job is pure trust — Brex must counter the "weren't you a startup card" association.

**3. Audience segmentation.** Three explicit tiers: **Startups**, **Mid-size companies**, **Enterprises**, each with their own copy and CTA. Startups: "complete financial stack designed to help start and scale." Mid-size: "Spend smarter worldwide with AI-powered budgets." Enterprise: "Simplify global travel and expenses with automation." This is the cleanest tier-segmentation on a homepage in the set — cleaner than Stripe's because it is all on the homepage, not spread across Solutions pages.

**4. Proof & credibility.** Stats: **35,000+ companies**, **up to 3.68% treasury yield**, **4,250 hours annual time savings**, **99% policy compliance**, **up to 30x higher credit limits**, **up to $6M additional FDIC insurance**. The "up to 30x higher credit limits" is a competitive dagger — aimed straight at AmEx and banks. One named testimonial: **Mike Kim, VP Finance, DoorDash — "Brex has been a major gain for us in efficiency for T&E. It's easy for employees to understand where and how to spend."** Single most credible element: the DoorDash quote. A VP Finance at a public company does not put their name on a vendor unless it's working.

**5. Product demonstration.** Heavy — cards, dashboards, budget-approval flows, travel UI all shown. Brex is a product-led company and the homepage reflects it.

**6. Navigation architecture.** Five top-level: Products | Solutions | Resources | Customers | Pricing. The Products mega-menu splits into Products (Card, Banking, Expenses, Accounting, Bill Pay, Travel, Startups, Release notes) and Platform (Intelligence, Global ready, Native integrations, Live Budgets, Reporting, Mobile app, API). Notable: Brex has a "Customers" nav item — most B2B fintechs bury case studies.

**7. Distinctive copy & rhetorical patterns.** Signature lines: **"The card is just the start."** **"Control spend before it happens."** **"Make doing your expenses easy."** **"Accelerate accounting with AI."** The rhetorical move is *pre-emptive correction*: "the card is just the start" is Brex explicitly disavowing its origin. Vocabulary: "speed and control," "modern," "AI-powered," "worldwide," "automation." Brex is compound-term-heavy — they construct noun phrases ("live budgets," "policy compliance") that sound like proprietary features even when the concept is generic.

**8. What they don't do.** No consumer-facing copy (Brex explicitly does not serve individuals). No dev-focused API hero (the API is in a sub-nav, not the front). No founder-story content. No ad for the Brex exit/saga. Opinionated signal: Brex wants to be judged as an enterprise finance platform, full stop.

**9. Second-order.** Brex is actually selling to the *Finance leader*, not the employee or founder. Every piece of copy is ROI- and control-oriented. Category: *"enterprise spend management"* — they are competing with SAP Concur and Coupa on one side, Stripe on the other. Emotional register: crisp, credible, faintly corporate. The pacing favors the tire-kicker: you can self-serve to signup or book a demo. Mobile design shows the same tiered structure with the size-tier tabs becoming a swipe-carousel.

## 5. Plaid — plaid.com

**1. Hero dissection.** Headline: **"Turn data into revolutionary financial products."** Subhead: **"Connect to real-time insights on the Plaid Network to create fast, safe,"** (truncated — the full line is "fast, safe, and more personalized financial experiences"). CTAs: "Talk to our team" (contact-sales anchor) and "Start building" (to developer dashboard). Six-word headline; "revolutionary" does a lot of work but risks hyperbole. Framing pattern is *capability-promise with network language*. Tone: confident and a touch breathless. Visual: a Benjamin Franklin illustration with an animated winking expression and rainbow gradient effects — extremely un-banky. Plaid's brand has always played with money iconography, and the $100-bill face is their anchor.

**2. Section 2.** Heading: **"Intelligent finance is taking shape."** Tagged to a conference CTA: "Save your spot at Effects 26." This is unusual — the second section is an event ad, not a product section. It signals Plaid's shift from "API for banking data" to "platform defining the future of finance."

**3. Audience segmentation.** Developers, financial institutions, fintech builders, enterprises, and consumers — five routes. The Solutions dropdown has 9 use cases (Open Finance, Fraud Prevention, KYC/AML, etc.) plus 11 industries. Developer is the primary, but Plaid has been widening aggressively to enterprise banks.

**4. Proof & credibility.** Stats: **1 in 2 banked U.S. adults use Plaid**, **25% higher conversion**, **1 million daily connections**, **20 countries**, **12K banks connected**. Most credible element: the "1 in 2 banked U.S. adults" figure, though it invites scrutiny. No named customer testimonials with full attribution on the homepage. Social-proof via transactional visuals showing Venmo, Carvana, Robinhood — product-in-context logo treatment.

**5. Product demonstration.** Moderate. Animated product cards show onboarding, payments, fraud, credit, open finance. No interactive demo. The visual language is *network* — lines, nodes, flows — which reinforces the "Plaid Network" positioning.

**6. Navigation architecture.** Six top-level: Products | Solutions | Developers | Resources | Pricing | Log in / Contact sales. "Contact sales" is adjacent to "Log in" — parity, rather than primary CTA. The Developers nav is given first-class treatment (not buried).

**7. Distinctive copy & rhetorical patterns.** Signature lines: **"A network that gets more powerful daily,"** **"Want access to 12K banks? We've got the (API) keys,"** **"More customers. Less fraud. Higher revenue."** The "(API) keys" pun is light but intentional — it signals the brand has a sense of humor, a rarity in fintech infrastructure. The recurring frame is *network effects*: Plaid is not selling you an API, they're selling you access to a growing network.

**8. What they don't do.** No traditional testimonials with names and titles. No dense logo wall — they use contextual transaction visuals instead. No above-fold pricing. No founder photos. No "team" section on the homepage. The opinionated signal: Plaid sells a network, and networks don't need individual endorsers.

**9. Second-order.** Plaid is selling to two increasingly separated buyers — the fintech developer integrating Plaid Link, and the bank CTO deciding whether to let Plaid pull their data. The homepage straddles both, slightly uneasily. Category: *"open finance infrastructure"* — Plaid is trying to redefine itself from "bank data API" to the Visa of open banking. Emotional register: optimistic, slightly nervous, heavier on the future than the present. Pacing is conference-seasonal — the page visibly rotates around Plaid's Effects conference.

## 6. Adyen — adyen.com

Built to scale with the world's leading businesses."** CTAs: "Talk to our team" and "Contact us" — both sales paths, no self-serve. **2. Section 2.** Heading: **"Run your business with confidence."** Copy: "Adyen delivers the control, reliability, and expertise global enterprises depend on." This is the enterprise comfort-blanket section: nothing about growth, everything about *not failing*. Adyen knows its buyer is defensive, not offensive.

**4. Proof & credibility.** Stats: **€1.4T processed annually**, **99.999% uptime**, **150+ currencies**, **200+ local payment methods**, **29 global offices**. Logos: Adobe, Prada Group, Vagaro, Nord Security, L'Occitane, Meta, Rectangle Health. No named testimonials with titles — Adyen is too corporate for that. The single most credible proof: €1.4T + US/UK/EU banking licenses. They are the only company in the payments set to hold all three.

**5. Product demonstration.** Low. Adyen is not product-led; they are trust-led. The page is mostly text and icons. There are no dashboard screenshots of substance.

**6. Navigation architecture.** Five top-level: Products | Businesses we serve | About | Resources | Pricing. "Businesses we serve" is the signature phrasing — it's Adyen's equivalent of "Solutions," but more austere. No mega-menu chaos.

**7. Distinctive copy & rhetorical patterns.** Signature lines: **"Global scale. Local expertise. Powered by trillions of euros in transaction data."** **"When certainty matters, Adyen's the platform of choice."** **"If you're building for scale, there's no alternative."** The rhetorical move is *enterprise absolutism* — each sentence is a closing argument. "No alternative" is the most aggressive claim on any homepage in the set, and Adyen is confident enough to make it.

**8. What they don't do.** No self-serve signup. No developer-first content. No startup band. No testimonial quotes. No consumer-facing copy. No gamification, no product tour, no "free tier." Opinionated signal: Adyen is a B2B enterprise company; everything else is a distraction.

**9. Second-order.** Adyen is selling to the VP of Payments at a global retailer who has a Stripe contract in hand and a procurement department to please. The entire page is designed to pass an RFP review. Category: *"enterprise payments platform"* — explicitly not "fintech infrastructure" (Stripe's framing); the pun in the headline even subtly distances Adyen from "fintech" by reclaiming "bank." Emotional register: European, patrician, slightly cold. Pacing is slow — no urgency — because Adyen sells into nine-month enterprise cycles.

## 8. Wise — wise.com

**1. Hero dissection.** Headline: **"Money for here, there and everywhere."** Subhead: **"160 countries and territories. 40 currencies. Get the account built to save you money round the world."** CTAs: "Open an account" and "Send money now." Seven-word headline — playful, borderline childlike, an echo of "here, there and everywhere" (Beatles). Tone: friendly, informal, consumer-first. Visual is surprisingly minimal — typography-forward, no hero image in the markup. Wise is the only fintech-infrastructure-adjacent site in the set that goes typography-only in the hero. Strategic job: frame Wise as the intuitive answer to cross-border money — not a payments API, but a *bank account you hold*.

**2. Section 2.** Heading: **"Trusted by millions moving billions."** Copy: "We move R279 billion worldwide every month." Plus "4.3/5 from 200,000+ reviews" and "Get help from thousands of specialists any time over email, phone and chat." This is Wise's trademark: combine volume, ratings, and *human service* in a single trust block. The "thousands of specialists" line is deliberately non-tech — Wise knows its consumer skeptical of app-only services.

**3. Audience segmentation.** Personal, Business, Platform — three top-level routes. Wise's platform business is a real enterprise offering (powering Monzo, N26, etc.) but it's the third route, not the first. Target segments include international professionals, students, families split across countries, freelancers, SMBs needing cross-border payroll.

**4. Proof & credibility.** Stats: **R279B/month** (localized ZAR), **4.3/5 Trustpilot**, **160 countries**, **40 currencies**, **700K+ businesses**. # PART TWO — CROSS-CUTTING PATTERNS

## A. What 15+ of these sites agree on

**Hero compression.** Almost every best-in-class hero in the set is under 10 words. Stripe (7), Ramp (4), Mercury (3), Linear (10), Morpho (7), Wise (7), Notion (6), Figma (6), Raycast (5). The outliers — Anthropic (11), Checkout (5 + 35-word subhead) — break the rule deliberately. The consensus: *the fewer words, the more the reader trusts the claim*.

**Two CTAs, one primary, one secondary, no more.** The pattern is overwhelming: Stripe (Get started / Sign up), Mercury (Open account / Launch demo), Linear (Get started / Contact sales), Vercel (Start Deploying / Get a Demo), Notion (Get Notion free / Request a demo), Morpho (Launch App / Contact us). The variation is on the secondary: interactive demo (Mercury), video-demo (Brex), contact-sales (Linear). Almost no one uses three hero CTAs. The pattern suggests visitors cannot cognitively evaluate more than two paths in a first-second scan.

**Subhead as the rescue net.** Heroes are compressed *because* the subhead is doing the specificity work. Stripe's hero "Financial infrastructure to grow your revenue" is too abstract alone; the subhead "from your first transaction to your billionth" disambiguates. Mercury's "Radically different banking" is reinforced by "Apply online in 10 minutes." The pattern: *abstract claim, concrete proof, in that order, always*.

**Logos in Section 2 or Section 3, rarely in the hero itself.** Brex, Notion, Checkout, Figma, Stripe, Framer all place logos in the second or third section. Exceptions: Morpho places partners very high (because its partners *are* the pitch). Mercury skips logo walls entirely in favor of named founder testimonials. Almost no one puts a logo wall *above* the fold in 2026 — that was a 2018-2021 pattern.

**Verbatim testimonials from named peers.** The most effective testimonials share a pattern: (1) one or two short sentences, (2) a specific name and role, (3) a recognizable company. Linear's Ramp/OpenAI/Opendoor quotes, Framer's five Head-of-Design quotes, Mercury's founder quotes, Raycast's Guillermo Rauch quote — all fit this pattern. Absent: anonymous testimonials with first-name-only attribution (the consumer pattern, e.g., Wise, Chime) — enterprise buyers treat these as noise.

**One numeric proof point that is unanswerable.** Stripe ($1.9T), Mercury (1 in 3 startups), Notion (62% of Fortune 100), Plaid (1 in 2 banked US adults), Adyen (€1.4T), Brex (35,000+ companies). Each site has one stat designed to end the "should we take them seriously?" question. The stat is usually placed in Section 3, not the hero — because it means more when it arrives after the claim has been made.

**Product-shown-not-told.** Linear, Mercury, Brex, Supabase, Figma, Framer, Raycast all put significant product UI on the homepage. Adyen and Anthropic are the notable exceptions — both lean on text and brand authority.

**Trust-layer language specifically calibrated to buyer anxiety.** Mercury: "Standard protection stops short. Mercury goes further." Adyen: "Control, reliability, and expertise global enterprises depend on." Linear: "Security" as a top-nav item. Notion: "SOC 2, ISO, HIPAA" sub-footer. Every B2B site in the set has an enterprise-trust block; the specific language is tuned to what the buyer is worried about.

**Dedicated AI positioning in 2026.** 18 of 25 sites have a visible AI framing — not as a feature, as a reposition. Linear ("Designed for the AI era"), Notion ("AI workspace"), Vercel ("AI Cloud"), Figma ("Prompt, code, design"), Stripe ("Agentic commerce"), Anthropic (entire brand). The sites without AI framing are the ones most tied to pre-AI categories (Wise, Adyen, Morpho) or to audiences that don't care (Chime, Monzo).

**Mega-menus are dead (for new SaaS); tight nav is in.** Linear, Raycast, Morpho, Anthropic — all have tight navs under 8 top-level items. The heavy mega-menus (Stripe, Adyen, Mercury) persist only for companies with genuinely large product portfolios. New entrants deliberately reduce navigation surface to signal focus.

**"Pricing" is increasingly in top nav.** Stripe, Ramp, Mercury, Brex, Linear, Vercel, Supabase, Notion, Framer, Raycast — all have Pricing in the top nav. The sites that don't are either enterprise-only (Adyen, Coinbase Institutional, Plaid, Morpho) or consumer (Wise, Chime, Monzo). The directional signal: transparency is now a trust move, not a leak.

## B. What distinguishes the best from the middle-tier

The best sites — Linear, Stripe, Mercury, Raycast, Morpho, Ramp — share a quality called **category authorship**. They are not "the best X" in an existing category; they are naming or renaming the category itself.

- Linear: "A new species of product tool" — refusing to be compared to Jira.
- Stripe: "financial infrastructure" — not "payments."
- Mercury: "banking built on software" — not "neobank."
- Raycast: "your shortcut to everything" — not "launcher."
- Morpho: "universal lending network" — not "DeFi protocol."
- Ramp: "time is money. Save both" — not "spend management."

Middle-tier sites describe *what they do* within an existing category. Top-tier sites redefine the category so the comparison no longer applies. This is the single most transferable insight for Kamino.

Second: **the best heroes state a claim that is either true or obviously falsifiable**. Vague heroes (Vercel's "best web experiences," Plaid's "revolutionary") are weaker than concrete ones (Mercury's "10 minutes," Wise's "160 countries, 40 currencies," Anthropic's "400 meters on Mars").

Third: **the best sites respect the reader's intelligence**. Linear does not explain. Anthropic does not sell. Raycast does not pitch. The sites that feel cheap are the ones that over-explain or over-hedge — Plaid's truncated subhead, Vercel's bracket-rotation, generic "Built for scale" type lines. Top-tier sites leave space.

Fourth: **they all have a signature visual commitment**. Mercury's cinematic flow. Raycast's keyboard. Linear's dashboard. Supabase's Postgres green. Figma's color carousel. The commitment is deep — one idea, executed with conviction — rather than "hero illustration + product shot + logo wall," which is the middle-tier default.

Fifth: **distinctive brand-voice lines**. "Issue tracking is dead." "Somos incansáveis pra você não precisar ser." "Radically different banking." "Disappoint thieves." "It's not about saving time; it's about feeling like you're never wasting it." Middle-tier copy could be swapped between sites without changing meaning. Top-tier copy cannot.

## C. Anti-patterns common in less-effective sites

**Adjective stacking in the hero.** "Powerful, easy-to-use, secure, scalable." This is the B2B SaaS cliché and almost no best-in-class site in the set does it. The adjective is replaced by a concrete noun or a verb.

**Three CTAs with equal weight.** Creates paralysis. Best sites have one clear primary.

**Hero imagery unrelated to the product.** Abstract gradients that could belong to any SaaS. The best sites either show the product (Linear, Mercury, Brex) or commit to a branded visual motif (Raycast keyboard, Plaid Ben Franklin, Monzo's Hot Coral card).

**"Trusted by" headings with no numeric scale.** "Trusted by leading companies" is weaker than "Trusted by 35,000+ top companies" (Brex) or "1 in 3 startups choose Mercury."

**Rotating carousels of testimonials.** Top-tier sites place 3-5 fixed testimonials, because a carousel implies the content is interchangeable.

**"Learn more" as a primary CTA.** Signals the reader isn't ready to commit, which primes them not to.

**Cookie banners in prime real estate.** The best sites either use tiny compliance footers or handle consent without obscuring hero content.

## D. Category comparisons — where the fault lines are

**B2B fintech infrastructure** (Stripe, Adyen, Checkout, Plaid, Ramp, Brex, Mercury) — aggressive numeric proof, heavy enterprise vocabulary, "scale" as a centerpiece word, heroes that claim category ownership. Divided between two philosophies: **transparency/self-serve** (Stripe, Mercury, Ramp) versus **gated/enterprise-only** (Adyen, Coinbase Institutional). Mercury straddles, which is strategically risky but paying off.

**Consumer fintech/neobanks** (Revolut, Nubank, Monzo, Chime, Wise, SoFi) — emotional copy, lifestyle photography, simpler nav, trust badges over stats, rating-based credibility (Trustpilot, App Store). The best in this category (Monzo, Wise, Chime) lead with a single clear benefit and avoid product-feature listing. Revolut and SoFi fragment themselves with too many products.

**Modern B2B SaaS** (Linear, Vercel, Supabase, Figma, Notion, Framer, Raycast) — opinionated brand voice, dense product UI, founder/CEO testimonials, free-trial self-serve, tight navigation. The best are radically opinionated (Linear, Raycast); the middle are merely polished (Vercel, Framer). Supabase is unusual for coupling open-source stance with enterprise claims.

**AI-native** (Anthropic, Perplexity) — minimal copy, product-is-the-hero, research-led framing, refusal of conventional marketing tropes. Both win by *subtraction*.

**DeFi/crypto infrastructure** (Coinbase Institutional, Morpho, Ondo) — mixed. Coinbase Institutional adopts prime-brokerage vocabulary wholesale. Morpho and Ondo split the difference, using "infrastructure" and "institutional-grade" language while preserving onchain credibility. None of them flash APYs above the fold — a meaningful departure from DeFi summer patterns.

The fault lines: **B2B fintech** and **DeFi infrastructure** are converging rapidly. Morpho's homepage could, with minor copy edits, be a Stripe-esque pitch. Coinbase Institutional's page could pass for a Goldman Prime Services page. Kamino sits directly on this convergence — and the opportunity is to be the first to execute it with both Stripe-grade polish and credible institutional language.

## E. Copy patterns that recur (with examples)

**Verb-as-subject.** "Build and deploy" (Vercel). "Turn data into" (Plaid). "Accept payments, offer financial services" (Stripe). "Connect to" (Morpho). "Build in a weekend. Scale to millions" (Supabase). Verbs imply the reader is capable of doing the thing — it is an empowerment device.

**Pair construction.** "Time is money. Save both" (Ramp). "Build in a weekend. Scale to millions" (Supabase). "Finance built for speed and control" (Brex). "Min fees. Max ease. Full speed" (Wise). "Design bold. Launch fast" (Framer). The rhythm is memorable; the pair is a compressed story arc.

**Category-annexation.** "The AI workspace" (Notion). "The AI Cloud" (Vercel). "The answer engine" (Perplexity). "The universal lending network" (Morpho). Explicit "the + category" patterns claim uniqueness.

**Scale compression.** "From your first transaction to your billionth" (Stripe). "120+ countries" (Brex). "160 countries, 40 currencies" (Wise). "$1.9T processed in 2025" (Stripe). Scale is always stated numerically, never narratively.

**Contrastive framing.** "Serves, not extracts" (Morpho). "Banking's been a headache. Now, it's a head start" (Mercury). "It's not about saving time. It's about feeling like you're never wasting it" (Raycast). "Launch in weeks, not months" (Morpho). "No code limits, no handoffs" (Framer). This is the most powerful rhetorical pattern — it carries implicit competitive positioning.

**Specificity as voice.** "Hot Coral card" (Monzo). "Disappoint thieves" (Wise). "Night shift" (Notion). "Vibe coders" (Supabase). "Right opinions for fast-moving teams" (Kaz Nejatian quote on Linear). Concrete nouns and phrases that ONLY this brand could have written.

**Single-word claims.** "Obsess" (Checkout). "Radically" (Mercury). "Purpose-built" (Linear). "Universal" (Morpho). One heavy-lifting word per hero.

## F. Structural patterns that recur

**The five-section home.** The pattern repeats: (1) Hero, (2) Logos/Section-2 routing or big-claim, (3) Deep proof/stats, (4) Audience-or-use-case router, (5) Product deep-dive, (6) Closing CTA. Minor variations but the skeleton holds.

**The audience-tier band.** Brex's startup/mid-market/enterprise bands are the cleanest example. Stripe's Enterprises/Startups/Platforms section is the same pattern. The tier band is placed below the product claim — not above — because it addresses "is this for me?" after "what does it do?" has been answered.

**The editorial/press row.** "Mercury in the spotlight." Linear's "Now." Anthropic's news. This is a *recency signal* — it communicates aliveness and accelerating narrative. Kamino has a natural version of this in announcements like the FMA license and Andersen attestations.

**Footer as sitemap.** Every site in the set uses the footer for extensive navigation — it's where the mega-menu now lives for sites that rejected mega-menus above.

# PART THREE — PRINCIPLES

## 1. Lead with category, not feature.

The best heroes name or annex a category. Linear is not "project management"; it is "the product development system." Stripe is "financial infrastructure," not "a payments API." Kamino's locked "Connecting global capital to the new financial system" is already operating at this altitude — the rest of the page must sustain it. Avoid degrading to "on-chain lending platform" anywhere above the fold.

*Examples: Linear, Stripe, Mercury.*

## 2. One primary CTA. One secondary. Never three.

Cognitive load studies have been running since Hick's Law, but the modern set confirms it empirically. The best pages force one choice: I'm ready (primary), or I need more (secondary). The secondary is often a demo — interactive (Mercury), video (Brex), or sales-call (Linear). A third CTA reliably dilutes both.

*Examples: Mercury, Linear, Notion.*

## 3. Concrete beats abstract every time.

"Radically different banking" works *because* "Apply online in 10 minutes" follows it. "The best web experiences" is weak because "6x faster shipping" doesn't rescue it with enough specificity. Concreteness is not a feature list — it is one precise fact that pins the claim to reality.

*Examples: Mercury ("10 minutes"), Wise ("160 countries, 40 currencies"), Anthropic ("400 meters on Mars").*

## 4. Proof lives in Section 2 or 3 — rarely the hero.

The hero is for the claim. The proof is the reader's reward for scrolling. Putting a logo wall above the fold dilutes the hero's claim by pre-empting its need to earn trust. Instead, use Section 2 for either (a) a named testimonial from a peer, (b) a logo wall with a specific scale claim, or (c) a single unarguable stat.

*Examples: Stripe (Section 3 stats), Brex ("35,000+ companies" in Section 2), Mercury ("1 in 3 startups" mid-page).*

## 5. Segment audiences explicitly — but only after establishing the core claim.

The best multi-audience sites don't fragment the hero. Brex, Stripe, Mercury all run a single brand hero, then surface audience-specific bands lower on the page. Kamino's four audiences (Individuals, Institutions, Issuers, Fintechs) should NOT appear as four boxes in the hero — they should appear as a mid-page router, after "Connecting global capital to the new financial system" has done its work.

*Examples: Brex (startup/mid/enterprise tiers), Stripe (Enterprises/Startups/Platforms bands).*

## 6. Testimonials must be named, titled, and short.

The "faceless first-name-from-Denmark" pattern (Wise) works for consumer fintech, not institutional finance. The enterprise pattern is: full name + specific role + logo'd company + one or two sharp sentences. Kamino should pursue testimonials from named CIOs, CFOs, treasury managers — not quotes from unidentified "institutional clients."

*Examples: Linear (Gabriel Peal, OpenAI), Framer (five named Heads of Design), Brex (Mike Kim, VP Finance, DoorDash).*

## 7. Show the product if you have one. Show the brand if you don't.

Linear, Mercury, Brex, Supabase, Framer all put product UI above the fold because their product is the argument. Anthropic and Morpho don't, because their product is *infrastructure* that lives in someone else's app. Kamino's vault product has a beautiful UI — it should appear on the homepage. But the institutional dashboard product is still being built; for that audience, lean on brand trust.

*Examples: Linear (dashboard), Mercury (cinematic flow), Anthropic (typographic restraint).*

## 8. Trust furniture is earned, not assumed. And placement matters.

Security badges, audit logos, regulatory credentials belong in a dedicated trust block mid-to-late page — not in the hero. The hero is for claims. Kamino's FMA license, Andersen UK attestation, 20 audits, zero bad debt, Haruko pipeline, and qualified custodians are all powerful — but they should be concentrated in a "Standard protection stops short. Kamino goes further"-style section (to steal Mercury's frame), not scattered into the hero.

*Examples: Mercury ("Standard protection stops short..."), Morpho (audits list), Adyen (banking licenses).*

## 9. Use contrast-pairs and antithesis for rhetorical power.

"Serves, not extracts." "Launch in weeks, not months." "Banking's been a headache. Now, it's a head start." "It's not about saving time; it's about feeling like you're never wasting it." Contrast creates memorability and implicitly positions against a named competitor.

*Examples: Morpho, Mercury, Raycast.*

## 10. Numbers win where adjectives lose.

Replace "high uptime" with "99.999%." Replace "large volume" with "$1.9T in 2025." Replace "many customers" with "1 in 3 startups." Kamino has great numbers ($3B AUM, $19B loans originated, zero bad debt, 3+ years, 20 audits). They should appear exactly, with units.

*Examples: Stripe, Adyen, Notion.*

## 11. Avoid defining yourself by negation.

"We're not another DeFi protocol" reads as insecure. "This isn't yield farming" anchors the reader on yield farming. The rule is positive construction: "Institutional lending, onchain" beats "Not another DeFi yield farm." None of the top-tier sites define themselves by what they are not.

*Examples: Anthropic ("safety at the frontier"), Morpho ("universal lending network"), Linear ("a new species").*

## 12. AI framing is now table stakes for any technology company — but only if it's integrated, not bolted-on.

Linear annexed "AI era" into their hero seamlessly. Notion re-positioned the entire brand as "The AI workspace." Vercel coined "The AI Cloud." But Plaid's AI framing is mostly decorative; so is Supabase's. The principle: if AI is genuinely re-shaping your product, claim it at the hero altitude. If it's a feature, keep it as a feature. Don't fake it.

*Examples: Linear (integrated), Notion (re-positioned), Stripe (agentic commerce as a solution track).*

## 13. Opinionated voice reads as credibility.

"Issue tracking is dead." "Obsess over every basis point." "Open by default, secure by design." "No ads. No sponsored content." Strong opinions are trust signals; neutral copy reads as committee-authored. Kamino has a distinctive voice available — regulated, conservative, institutional — but it should be *chosen*, not *defaulted-to*.

*Examples: Linear, Anthropic, Raycast.*

## 14. Footer as sitemap; hero as stance.

Keep the hero clean. Push breadth to the footer. Every top-tier site has a dense, organized footer with 6-10 columns. Kamino can have an enormous footer — regulatory disclosures, audit logs, contact, docs, career, press — without clutter, because readers who scroll that far are investigating.

*Examples: Stripe (8 columns), Adyen (7), Notion (6).*

## 15. Pricing transparency is now a credibility play.

A decade ago, "Contact sales for pricing" signaled enterprise seriousness. Today, it reads as hiding something. The top-tier sites have Pricing in the top nav — and route contact-sales for enterprise contract negotiation only. For Kamino, the question is whether vault APYs should be visible on the homepage (verdict: yes, but with institutional context — 6-8% APY, overcollateralized, supervised, custodian-held).

*Examples: Stripe, Linear, Brex.*

# PART FOUR — SHARP INSIGHTS AND CONTRARIAN TAKES

## Most reviewers miss the "category-authorship" move.

People reviewing landing pages usually focus on tactics — button colors, above-the-fold optimization, CTA placement. They miss that the top-tier sites are almost all doing something strategic: they are *coining* or *renaming* a category in the hero. "AI Cloud" (Vercel). "Answer engine" (Perplexity). "AI workspace" (Notion). "Universal lending network" (Morpho). "A new species of product tool" (Linear). "Financial infrastructure" (Stripe). This is not branding; it is the positioning decision made visible in copy. Kamino's hero "Connecting global capital to the new financial system" is in this tradition — but the next three sections must keep authoring the category ("institutional onchain credit," "regulated onchain yield," "the onchain credit network" — something Kamino owns).

## The Mercury + Morpho precedent for Kamino.

If redesigning Kamino's landing page, the two closest spiritual predecessors in the set are **Mercury** (for tone, founder-voice credibility, and product-led demo) and **Morpho** (for infrastructure-level positioning and enterprise-connection emphasis). Mercury shows how to make a regulated financial product feel like software. Morpho shows how to make an onchain primitive feel institutional. Kamino needs both.

## The "launch-in-weeks, not months" rhetorical move is underused in DeFi.

Morpho uses it. Every other crypto site on the set buries integration time or obscures it. For Kamino's Issuers and Fintechs audiences, a crisp "launch in weeks" claim would be differentiating.

## Counter-intuitive: institutional buyers *do* want product UI on the homepage.

The received wisdom is that institutional sites should look like Goldman Sachs — austere, typographic, no product. But Coinbase Institutional, BlackRock's digital-assets page, and Morpho all show product/dashboard imagery. The reason: the institutional buyer is often a crypto-native person, and they expect to see the actual product. The era of "whitepaper in the hero" is over.

## The "named research initiative" move is extremely effective and almost no one else does it.

Anthropic's "Project Glasswing" second-section is a surprisingly strong pattern. It communicates *we are doing things in the world* without making a product claim. Kamino has genuine material for this — the FMA license, the Andersen attestation, the Haruko pipeline are "projects" in the same narrative register. Treating them as named, dated initiatives rather than buried bullet points would set Kamino apart from every DeFi peer.

## The signature physical object is a persistent brand anchor.

Monzo's Hot Coral card. Raycast's keyboard. Nubank's purple card. Plaid's Franklin. Apple's hardware. A physical (or quasi-physical) object becomes an anchor for trust. Kamino doesn't have one — and that's a potential gap. An institutional-vault visual anchor (maybe the vault itself as a 3D object, or the BTC-in-custody visualization) could serve this role.

## The AI-agent shift is real and every B2B site has adapted.

Linear's "teams and agents." Notion's "Night shift" agents. Stripe's "agentic commerce." These are not features in a list — they are first-sentence pivots. The shift this year: every serious B2B brand is explicitly welcoming agents as users. Kamino should have a stance on whether agents can interact with its vaults (via API), and if yes, a front-page acknowledgment.

## What feels fresh and untried across the 25.

(1) Interactive demo as a primary CTA (Mercury is the only fintech doing this well). (2) Named-research-initiative as a second section (Anthropic). (3) Contrast-pair copy that directly names a category-villain without mentioning them (Morpho, Raycast). (4) The use of scale-ratios over absolute numbers ("1 in 3 startups" is stickier than "300K users"). (5) The Stripe case-study pattern where one-line quotes are paired with a thick metric ("160 countries | 11K+ locations"). (6) The Linear "Now" nav item as a living changelog. (7) Pricing with institutional detail in-nav.

## What is "this year's pattern" vs. what is timeless.

*This year (2026):* "AI era" language; agents as first-class users; pricing transparency; interactive demos; named research initiatives; category-authoring heroes.

*Timeless:* Concrete numbers over adjectives; named testimonials with roles; contrast-pair rhetoric; tight hero copy; product-is-the-pitch when you have a product; opinionated voice.

## The best landing page in the set.

**Linear** does it best. The hero "The product development system for teams and agents" compresses three strategic moves — category authorship ("the system"), audience-defining insertion ("and agents"), and subtle AI-era repositioning — into ten words. The second section "A new species of product tool" commits. The testimonials are peer-to-peer. The product UI is the argument. The footer is disciplined. Every element reinforces the same single claim: *this is the operating system for the way modern teams actually work*. No word is wasted. No visual is there for decoration. It is the most coherent integration of copy, visual, and strategy in the set.

**Runner-up: Mercury.** For different reasons — it's the best example of making a regulated product feel like a modern software company, with rhythm in the copy ("Banking's been a headache. Now, it's a head start") and discipline in the proof ("1 in 3 startups").

**Sleeper pick for Kamino: Morpho.** Because the template is nearly perfect for what Kamino needs — infrastructural claim, enterprise partners shown early, institutional security without losing crypto-native credibility, and a refusal to flash APYs. If Kamino can be "Morpho plus Mercury," with regulatory depth neither one currently has, there is a defensible and differentiated landing-page identity available.

# PART FIVE — BASELINE RECOMMENDATIONS FOR KAMINO (brief, pre-synthesis)

Given the research, the landing-page architecture Kamino could pursue is:

**Hero:** Keep "Connecting global capital to the new financial system" — it is in the category-authorship tradition. Pair it with ONE concrete proof-subhead: for example, *"$3B+ AUM. $19B in loans originated. Zero bad debt. Three years live."* Two CTAs: Explore Vaults / Talk to our team.

**Section 2 — The unarguable stat:** One big number, prominently. The single most credible element in Kamino's deck: **"Zero bad debt on $19B+ originated."** Nothing else is as hard to argue with.

**Section 3 — Audience router:** A clean four-band layout (Individuals / Institutions / Issuers / Fintechs), each with tailored copy and a distinct CTA.

**Section 4 — The trust layer ("Standard protection stops short. Kamino goes further"):** FMA supervision, Andersen UK attestation, 20 audits, Haruko pipeline, qualified custodians. Concrete, dated, named.

**Section 5 — Institutional Yield deep-dive:** Product-led. Show the vault. 6-8% APY with context. BTC collateral at named custodians. Overcollateralized. Monthly attestations.

**Section 6 — Named testimonials:** If achievable, two or three institutional names with titles.

**Section 7 — Recency strip:** FMA license announcement, most recent Andersen attestation, latest vault launch. A living editorial strip, Linear-"Now" style.

**Footer:** Very dense. Disclosures, jurisdictions, audit logs, docs, contact.

Everything described in the research is transferable. The challenge for Kamino is neither visual nor verbal — it is *integration*. The 25 sites that work best are the ones where the hero, proof, product, and voice are all saying the same thing. Kamino's hero already says the right thing. The rest of the page needs to follow.
